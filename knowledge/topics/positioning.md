# Product positioning and segmentation

## Current consensus

April Dunford's framing is the corpus-canonical model for positioning. Reinforced by Arielle Jackson, Andy Raskin, and Christopher Lochhead.

### April Dunford's 5 components of positioning
1. **Competitive alternatives** — what do customers use today (including doing nothing)?
2. **Unique attributes** — what features only your product has?
3. **Value (and proof)** — what value do those unique attributes deliver, with evidence?
4. **Target market** — the segment that values this most.
5. **Market category** — the frame that makes the value obvious.

The framework's core insight: **positioning is a set of context-setting decisions, not a tagline**. Most "positioning" work is logo + slogan, not actual positioning.

### Common positioning failure modes
- **Frame the product against the wrong category.** A category-creator placed in a familiar category sounds derivative; placed in a new category, it sounds visionary.
- **Position to everyone.** "Our product is for everyone" guarantees no one feels addressed.
- **Lead with features.** Features are commodity; alternatives + value + category make the difference.
- **Not testing positioning with real customers.** Positioning that survives sales calls is real; positioning that lives only in marketing decks dies on contact.

### Strategic narrative (Andy Raskin)
A complementary frame: positioning as story. Problem → enemy → vision → evidence → CTA. The "killer sales pitch" structure (Lenny + Andy Raskin).

### Category creation (Christopher Lochhead, "Play Bigger")
For some products, positioning means creating a new category rather than competing in an existing one. Higher upside; harder to execute; requires sustained category-building investment.

### What's changed in 2025-2026
- **AI features have repositioned everyone.** Many B2B SaaS companies have rewritten positioning to lead with AI. This is sometimes correct (when AI delivers real differentiation) and sometimes lazy (when AI is bolted on).
- **AEO + LLM citations** (cf. [ai-search-aeo](ai-search-aeo.md)) have raised the stakes for positioning clarity. Vague positioning gets summarized away by answer engines; sharp positioning gets cited.

## What this means for non-PM consumers

- **If you're a CEO defining positioning**: this is your job, not your CMO's. Positioning decisions cut across product, sales, and marketing.
- **If you're a CMO**: April Dunford's framework + Andy Raskin's narrative pattern are the two highest-leverage tools.
- **If you're a sales lead frustrated by losing on positioning**: the fix is at the strategy level, not in the deck. Push the problem upstream.
- **If you're an exec reviewing a marketing pitch**: ask "who is this not for?" If the team can't answer, the positioning isn't sharp.
