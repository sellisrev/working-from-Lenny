# 7 Powers — Hamilton Helmer

## Consensus key insights

Helmer's taxonomy of business moats. The seven powers + when each applies.

### The 7 powers
1. **Scale economies**: cost advantage from being big. Examples: Amazon, Costco. Required: high fixed costs and big enough market.
2. **Network effects**: each user makes the product more valuable to other users. Examples: Facebook, LinkedIn, marketplaces. Required: a network where value-per-user increases with size.
3. **Counter-positioning**: incumbents can't copy you because doing so would damage their existing business. Examples: Vanguard vs. active managers, Netflix DVD vs. Blockbuster. Required: a real conflict in the incumbent's economics.
4. **Switching costs**: customers can't easily move. Examples: Salesforce, Snowflake, integrated platforms. Required: integration depth or contract structure.
5. **Branding**: durable price premium from brand reputation. Examples: Apple, Tiffany. Required: consistent brand investment over years.
6. **Cornered resource**: exclusive access to something competitors lack. Examples: Pixar's brain trust, ASML's EUV lithography. Required: actual exclusivity.
7. **Process power**: ways of working that competitors can't replicate. Examples: Toyota production system. Required: deep, hard-to-copy organizational practices.

### Two key insights
- **Power = Benefit × Barrier.** A power is durable because it provides a benefit AND has a barrier preventing competition. Without the barrier, the benefit erodes to zero.
- **Most companies have at most 1-2 powers.** The exercise is to be honest about which.

## Potentially interesting but rarer takes

**[CRITICAL READING REQUIRED]**

- **"7 is the right number of powers."** Helmer himself acknowledges the taxonomy may evolve. Treat the framework as a useful checklist, not a fixed truth.
- **"Process power is real."** Roger Martin (in his own episode) is somewhat skeptical of process power as durable; argues most "process power" examples decay over time. Worth holding both views.
- **Distribution as a power** (Evan Spiegel 2026 framing). Spiegel argues distribution is the most important moat in 2026; Helmer's framework would absorb this as some combination of scale economies + branding + cornered resource depending on the case.

## Where the consensus may have decayed

- **For AI products**, the 7-powers framework still applies but specific examples are still emerging. Data-as-moat (cf. Shaun Clowes on AI products being all about the data) maps to cornered resource + scale economies.
- **The "moat from foundation model access" question** is unresolved in the corpus. Helmer's framework doesn't have a clean fit; possibly cornered resource for the labs themselves, less clear for downstream products.
